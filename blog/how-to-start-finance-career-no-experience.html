How to start a career in finance with no experience

A realistic roadmap for entering finance from any background — the doors that actually open, the skills that transfer, and a 90-day plan.

6-minute read

Nobody is born with finance experience, and most people working in finance did not start there. The industry runs on a quiet pipeline of career changers — former teachers, retail managers, military veterans, customer-service reps — who walked in through side doors and learned the machinery from the inside. This is a practical guide to doing the same: which doors open, what to learn first, and how to spend the next 90 days.

1. Start with the doors that are actually open

Not every finance job is reachable on day one, and that is fine. Investment-banking analyst programs and quantitative trading roles recruit from narrow pipelines with strict credential filters. But the industry is enormous, and several large functions hire for traits you can prove from any background: operations, client service, trade support, fund administration, reconciliations, and corporate actions processing. These teams keep the financial system running — settling trades, moving cash, onboarding clients, fixing breaks — and they consistently hire people who have never worked in finance. The single biggest mistake career changers make is applying to “finance” as an abstract concept instead of to a specific desk whose daily work they can describe. Pick the desk first.

2. Translate your experience before you rewrite your résumé

Hiring managers in these functions are screening for three things: accuracy, deadline discipline, and clear written communication. Your current job almost certainly gave you all three — you just need to translate them into the industry’s language. A teacher who documented lesson plans and tracked thirty students’ progress was doing structured record-keeping and performance monitoring. A retail manager who counted the till and reconciled the end-of-day cash was doing cash reconciliation. Military after-action reports are exception reporting. Customer-support de-escalation is client communication under pressure. Write your résumé in the destination language, not the origin language, and run it through the platform’s résumé analyzer to check that the skills an operations hiring manager screens for are visible in the first half of the page.

3. Learn enough to sound credible in 30 days

You do not need a finance degree to get hired into operations or client service, but you do need to speak the language before interviews start. An hour a day for a month beats a weekend cram. Work through the platform’s 40 interactive modules starting with the trade lifecycle, settlement, corporate actions, and reconciliation — the concepts that come up in nearly every entry-level interview. Use the drills to lock in the vocabulary: settlement, fails, breaks, collateral, net asset value, trade date versus settlement date. The goal is not expertise; it is the ability to explain each term in plain English and connect it to a real workflow. An interviewer who hears you use “break” and “exception” correctly will stop caring what your degree was in.

4. Pick a lane using the role profiles

“I want to work in finance” is not a plan; “I want to be a reconciliations analyst” is. The platform’s 16 role profiles describe what each job actually involves day to day, the skills it requires, and indicative base-pay ranges by level and hub — read five or six of them and notice which daily work sounds like something you would be good at, not just something that sounds impressive. If you are still unsure, take the career-matching quiz on the homepage or talk it through with FinCoach, the on-device AI career coach that runs entirely in your browser with no account. People who pick a lane early learn faster and interview better, because every hour of preparation points at a specific job.

5. Build proof, not just credentials

Credentials have their place, but proof of work is what closes interviews for career changers. A hiring manager weighing two candidates will take the one who can already do the job’s core task. Use the platform’s calculators and drills to build fluency, then step into the securities-lending desk lab — a simulated desk day where you work through realistic scenarios the way an analyst would. Watch the narrated day-in-life videos to absorb the rhythm of the work: how mornings are triage, how deadlines are hard, how exceptions get investigated. Then write a one-page case study: “How I would investigate a reconciliation break.” That single page says more about your readiness than any summary line on a résumé.

6. Follow a realistic 90-day plan

Structure beats motivation. Weeks one and two: pick your lane and learn the vocabulary with the modules and the glossary. Weeks three through six: work the modules and drills daily until you can explain the core workflows without notes. Weeks seven through ten: build your proof project, rewrite your résumé in the industry’s language, and run it through the résumé analyzer. Weeks eleven and twelve: apply to roles in your lane and start informational conversations — ask people what their Tuesday looks like, and listen for the real hiring criteria. Expect the application phase to take months, and expect to start at an entry-level title even if you are senior in your current field. That is the normal price of learning a new industry’s plumbing from the inside.

The honest timeline

Most career changers need three to six months from serious start to signed offer, and the first role is usually a step down in title from where they are now. That is not a verdict on your ability — it is how every industry onboards outsiders. The people who rise fastest afterward are the ones who treat the entry role as paid training: learn the workflows, fix one process, and make your competence visible.

You do not need permission, a degree, or a connection to start. You need a lane, the vocabulary, and proof you can do the work — all three are learnable, and you can start today: browse the role profiles, open the modules, and ask FinCoach where someone with your background fits best.
